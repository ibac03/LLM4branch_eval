Structural Generalization · BA vs. home-domain policy

Where BA breaks

BA-evolved's branching policy solves its own domain well, and looks broadly strong against RR and WS at their standard test tiers. Push RR and WS to genuinely hard instances and the picture splits: against RR, BA's deficit against RR's own policy triples and BA starts timing out outright. Against WS, the deficit doesn't move at all.

At the easy tier, BA's policy is already behind both domains' own policies — 3.15× slower on RR, 2.16× slower on WS. That's the known cost of being a generalist. What changes under real difficulty is domain-specific: on RR, the gap nearly triples to 9.19× and BA starts failing instances it would have solved easily at the easy tier. On WS, the gap is statistically flat — 2.16× and 2.13× are the same number within noise — and BA never times out at either tier.

This lines up with a pattern seen in the reverse direction too: when RR- and WS-trained MLP policies were tested on BA's own large instances, WS-trained held up far better than RR-trained (24/30 vs 17/30 optimal). WS's denser, small-world structure is apparently closer to BA's dense preferential-attachment graphs than RR's sparse, regular structure is — in both directions. BA's apparent overall robustness in the main crosstest tables looks less like genuine domain-general strength and more like a size/structure-dependent artifact that happens to read as "robust" when the off-domain test tiers are easy.
